# Black Letter

A study app for 1L **Contracts**, **Torts**, and **Civil Procedure**.

More subjects get added as I take them — this grows with my coursework, so expect the
upper-level subjects to show up over the next couple of years. Existing decks also get
filled in as each course moves through new material.

Everything runs in your browser. There is no server, no account, and no data ever leaves
your device. Install it to your phone's home screen and it works offline.

---

## Install it on your phone

**iPhone**

1. Open this site in **Safari** — it has to be Safari, not Chrome.
2. Tap the **Share** button.
3. Scroll down and tap **Add to Home Screen**.

**Android**

1. Open the site in Chrome.
2. Menu (⋮) → **Add to Home screen**.

You'll get an icon that opens full-screen, with no address bar. Stay on wifi the first
time you open it so it can cache itself — after that it works with no signal.

You can also just use it in a normal browser tab on a laptop.

---

## Signing in

Type any email on the first screen. It is only a key for your saved progress — there is
no password, nothing is transmitted, and no one else can see what you've done. Your
progress is stored locally on the device you're using.

Because storage is local, progress does not sync between your phone and your laptop, and
clearing your browser's website data will erase it. Use **export** (top right) to save a
backup file, and **import** to restore it or move it to another device.

## The five modes

**Drill** — Mixed cards. Cloze deletions on rule language, flashcards, and scenario
multiple choice. Flashcards are self-rated: tap *I know it* or *Need help*. Asking for
help drops you to a fill-in-the-blank version of the same rule; getting that wrong sends
the card to your study pile. There's an *Actually missed it* button after a self-rated
card, for when you were being generous with yourself.

**Blurt** — Blank-page recall, which is the mode that matters most for essay exams. You
get a bare topic and five minutes to write everything you can produce from memory, notes
closed. Then a checklist of what the answer should have contained, which you honestly
self-score, followed by a model answer. Take the 90-second re-blurt when it's offered —
it's the step that confirms the gap actually closed.

**Ladder** — Element lists. It asks how many elements there are *before* showing you the
blanks, because knowing there are four is what stops you producing three on an exam and
never noticing. Grading is unordered and keyword-based, so phrase things however you'd
write them, and override any row the matcher gets wrong.

**Browse** — Read-only. Everything in the deck with answers visible, grouped by subject
and type, filterable to just your study pile. Nothing here changes your proficiency, so
it's safe for skimming before class.

**Attack** — The condensed one-page checklist per subject, sequenced the way you'd work
through an exam question. This is the page worth memorizing; use it as your list of blurt
topics.

**Progress** — Mastered / learning / study pile counts, accuracy across all graded
attempts, a 14-day activity chart, per-subject breakdowns, and a ranked list of what to
work on next.

## How the weighting works

Every item carries a proficiency score from 0 to 5. Getting something right moves it up;
missing it drops it straight to 0 — the study pile. Items are then drawn at random,
weighted so that a study-pile item comes up roughly 36 times more often than a mastered
one.

Nothing is ever retired. Mastered items keep resurfacing occasionally, because a rule you
nailed in September is exactly the kind of thing that has quietly evaporated by finals.

The **Mixed** chip draws across all three subjects at once. That's deliberate — having to
work out *which* framework applies before applying it is closer to an exam than drilling
one subject in a block, even though blocked practice feels smoother while you're doing it.

---

## What this is and isn't for

Flashcards build recall, not analysis. This deck exists so that rule statements and
element lists are automatic, which frees up your working memory on exam day for the part
that's actually graded: applying rules to facts you've never seen.

It is not a substitute for practice essays under time pressure. A reasonable split during
the semester is something like 20% of study time here, 30% reading and briefing, and 50%
practice problems — shifting hard toward practice exams in the weeks before finals.

## How this was built

Vibe coded. The app itself — every mode, the weighting, the offline setup — was written
with Claude, and the cards were generated from my own outlines, briefs, class notes, and
professors' slide decks rather than pulled from anywhere else. I reviewed the content as
it was produced, but it's a study aid built by a 1L with an AI, not a treatise.

---

## A caveat on the content

These cards were built from one student's outlines, class notes, and professors' slides.
Where a professor phrased a rule a particular way, that phrasing was kept, because that's
what gets graded in that class. Your professor may frame things differently, and courts
in your jurisdiction may come out the other way on genuinely contested points — the
material-benefit split between *Webb* and *Harrington* being the obvious example.

Check anything you plan to rely on against your own materials, and never cite this in
anything that matters.
